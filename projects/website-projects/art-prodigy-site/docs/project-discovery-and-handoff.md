## 1. Project origin

The artist is a close friend of the designer/developer from undergrad. He has been personally supportive in many ways, and this website is being created pro bono as an expression of care and appreciation.

The initial request from the artist was for something “fun and reflective of my personality,” while acknowledging that he does not yet know what that should look like.

His current public presence is primarily Instagram:

- Instagram: <https://www.instagram.com/art_prodigy05>
- Current handle: `@art_prodigy05`

The website may give his work an independent home that visitors can use without an Instagram account. It could eventually support commission inquiries, client work, original characters, custom products, or sales, depending on his goals.

## 2. Why the website may matter

The current Instagram account works as a social feed, but it mixes finished artwork, process material, reels, personal updates, and fitness content. A dedicated website could:

- Make the artwork accessible without requiring Instagram.
- Present a deliberately curated body of work.
- Separate portfolio work from personal/social posts without erasing his personality.
- Make commission inquiries easier and more consistent.
- Highlight his original creative voice alongside fan art.
- Give potential clients a professional link to share.
- Create room for future prints, merchandise, custom shoes, licensing, or collaborations.

These are opportunities, not yet confirmed goals.

## 3. Material reviewed

The initial assessment is based on:

- A text-message screenshot in which the artist asks for a fun site reflective of his personality.
- Four screenshots of the artist's Instagram grid and one expanded post.
- Visible examples of character illustration, fan art, pop-culture mashups, commemorative artwork, custom sneakers, comic-cover compositions, and personal content.

Instagram could not be reliably browsed without authentication, so the assessment is based on the screenshots supplied directly by the designer.

## 4. Initial visual and creative assessment

### Recurring qualities in the work

- Bold, expressive character design
- Strong silhouettes and action poses
- Saturated color and graphic outlines
- Comic-cover and poster-like compositions
- Cartoon, anime, gaming, and superhero influences
- Late-1990s and early-2000s nostalgia
- Saturday-morning-cartoon energy
- Pop-culture reinterpretations and unexpected mashups
- Black culture, representation, Juneteenth, and Black History Month themes
- Humor, warmth, and a sense of play
- Artwork applied to physical objects, including custom sneakers
- A visible personal creator presence rather than an anonymous studio identity

### Working positioning

The artist currently reads as a **character illustrator and pop-culture remix artist with a playful, nostalgic, Black-centered point of view**.

That sentence is an internal interpretation and must be tested with him. He may prefer different language or a broader identity.

### What the website should probably avoid

- A sterile white-box gallery that removes the personality from the work
- An interface so experimental that visitors cannot find the portfolio or commission form
- Copying the Instagram grid without adding curation or context
- Letting fan art completely overshadow original characters and original concepts
- Heavy animation that slows the site or competes with the illustrations
- Treating every social post as portfolio material

## 5. Proposed creative directions

### Direction A — The Saturday Morning Universe

**Current leading recommendation.**

The website behaves like the artist's own cartoon network, comic universe, or fictional broadcast channel. His illustrated self-portrait could welcome visitors, featured work could be “Now Showing,” and navigation could borrow lightly from TV channels or comic departments.

Potential ingredients:

- Illustrated self-portrait or original mascot in the hero
- Comic panels and bold portfolio cards
- Halftones, speed lines, ink borders, stickers, and paper texture
- Small character reactions and power effects on interaction
- A circular signature used as a stamp or loading mark
- Clear calls to view work and request a commission

The experience should feel playful on the surface while remaining structurally clear underneath.

### Direction B — Comic-Shop Archive

The portfolio is presented like a collection of comic issues, trading cards, arcade selections, or collectible covers. Visitors browse by subject or type.

Possible filters:

- Original characters
- Fan art and remixes
- Anime and gaming
- Cartoon nostalgia
- Black culture and commemorative work
- Custom sneakers
- Client and commission work

This direction may suit a large catalog and make browsing part of the experience.

### Direction C — Cartoon Network Meets Streetwear

A cleaner, more commercial structure using dark foundations, electric accent colors, oversized typography, stickers, crisp portfolio cards, and restrained motion.

This may be strongest if the main goal becomes commissions, apparel, prints, custom sneakers, or commercial illustration.

### Current recommendation

Combine the personality of **The Saturday Morning Universe** with the clarity of a professional illustration portfolio. Borrow archive or trading-card mechanics only where they improve browsing.

No direction is approved yet.

## 6. Preliminary visual language

**Proposed only:**

- Near-black or deep-navy foundation
- Electric cyan, superhero red, slime green, golden yellow, and lavender accents
- Heavy condensed display type paired with a highly readable sans serif
- Comic halftones, speed lines, paper grain, speech bubbles, and ink outlines
- Rounded controls inspired by retro television interfaces
- Motion used as seasoning: card tilt, glow, reaction, reveal, and page transition
- Artwork kept visually dominant over decorative interface elements

Final colors and typography should be derived from the artist's favorite work and personal preferences, not selected solely from this initial assessment.

## 7. Preliminary site structure

The first useful version could include:

1. **Home** — introduction, selected work, short artist statement, and clear calls to action
2. **Work** — curated and filterable portfolio
3. **Original Characters** — original worlds and character development
4. **Fan Art & Remixes** — clearly labeled transformative/pop-culture work
5. **Custom Kicks** — physical artwork and sneaker commissions, if still offered
6. **About** — biography, portrait, influences, personality, and creative process
7. **Commissions** — services, process, availability, FAQ, and inquiry form

This may be simplified for launch. For example, Work, About, and Commissions could be enough for version one.

## 8. Preliminary commission experience

If commissions are in scope, the initial website does not need automated pricing, checkout, or scheduling. A structured inquiry form would let the artist assess each request and issue a custom quote.

Potential form fields:

- Name and contact information
- Type of request: individual, couple, group, pet, original character, custom shoe, or other
- Description of the idea
- Desired style or example artwork
- Intended use: personal, gift, social, print, merchandise, or commercial
- Deadline or event date
- Budget range
- Reference-image upload
- Permission to share the finished work in the portfolio

Potential status language:

> Commissions: Open  
> Estimated turnaround: 3–5 weeks

The artist must define his actual pricing process, capacity, turnaround, revision policy, payment schedule, cancellation policy, and usage terms.

## 9. Portfolio curation recommendation

Instagram can remain the broad social record. The website should be edited more deliberately.

Proposed starting point:

- Homepage: 8–12 strongest and most representative pieces
- Full portfolio: approximately 20–30 selected pieces at launch
- Project pages: title, year, category, context, process, and final artwork where available
- About/process: selected personal photographs, studio material, sketches, and time-lapses
- Commission examples: finished work accompanied by a short description of the request

The artist should personally identify the work he considers strongest. Engagement counts should not make that decision for him.

## 10. Original work, fan art, and rights

Much of the visible portfolio reinterprets recognizable entertainment characters. Fan art can remain an authentic and valuable part of the portfolio, but the website should clearly distinguish it from original work and commercial client work.

Recommended safeguards:

- Label fan art and remixes accurately.
- Do not imply endorsement by or affiliation with the original rights holders.
- Do not offer commercial licenses for third-party characters.
- Confirm rights before selling prints or merchandise featuring protected characters.
- Give original characters prominent placement to strengthen the artist's independent identity.

This section is practical project guidance, not legal advice. Formal commercial use may require advice from a qualified intellectual-property professional.

## 11. Questions for the artist

The questions are ordered to keep the first conversation personal and imaginative before moving into operational details.

### A. Identity and ambition

1. When someone leaves your website, what are the three things you want them to remember about you?
2. How do you describe yourself as an artist when meeting someone for the first time?
3. Which three to five pieces feel most like **you**, regardless of likes or popularity?
4. What kinds of work do you most want to create over the next two years?
5. Do you want to be known primarily for character design, illustration, fan-art remixes, original stories, custom products, or something else?
6. Are there parts of your current Instagram work that no longer represent where you are going?
7. How important are Black culture, nostalgia, humor, gaming, anime, and cartoons to your public artistic identity?
8. What does the name “Art Prodigy” mean to you now? Do you want it to remain the main brand name?

### B. Purpose and audience

9. What is the most important job of the website: showcase your work, attract commissions, find commercial clients, sell products, build an audience, tell your story, or something else?
10. Who do you most hope visits: individual fans, people buying gifts, brands, art directors, publishers, convention organizers, sneaker clients, or collaborators?
11. If the site could create one new opportunity for you this year, what would you want it to be?
12. Should the site feel primarily like a professional portfolio, a personal creative universe, a shop, or a mixture?
13. Do you want people to follow your ongoing life and process, or encounter only a tightly curated body of work?

### C. Personality and visual direction

14. Which description feels most like you: **your own Saturday-morning cartoon universe**, **a collectible comic archive**, or **a cartoon-meets-streetwear studio**? What would you change?
15. Should the experience feel energetic and maximal, clean with playful moments, or somewhere between them?
16. What should visitors feel in the first ten seconds?
17. What colors feel like you? Are there colors you dislike or never want associated with your brand?
18. Which cartoons, games, comics, music, fashion, artists, or eras shaped your taste most?
19. Do you want your illustrated self-portrait to act as a host or guide throughout the site?
20. Would you enjoy playful interface ideas such as trading cards, TV channels, comic issues, hidden characters, or an arcade-style menu—or would that feel gimmicky?
21. How much motion do you want: mostly still, a few animated details, or a highly interactive experience?
22. Send three websites or creative projects you love and tell me exactly what you like about each one.
23. Send one or two websites you dislike and explain why.

### D. Artwork and organization

24. How would you naturally group your work?
25. Which pieces must appear on the homepage?
26. Which works are original characters or worlds that you own completely?
27. Do any original characters have names, stories, relationships, or settings we could introduce?
28. Should fan art have its own clearly labeled collection?
29. Do you have high-resolution exports, layered files, sketches, and process videos for your selected work?
30. Do you want custom sneakers treated as a separate service or simply included in the portfolio?
31. Are there private commissions you need permission to display?
32. Would you be willing to write or record a short story about selected projects?

### E. Commissions and services

33. Are you accepting commissions now? If not, do you expect to reopen them?
34. What kinds of commissions do you enjoy most?
35. What requests do you not want to accept?
36. Do you use fixed starting prices, custom quotes, or both?
37. What information do you need before deciding whether to accept a project?
38. What is your typical turnaround time?
39. How many revisions are included?
40. Do you require a deposit? When is the remaining balance due?
41. How should urgent requests be handled?
42. Do personal and commercial usage have different prices or terms?
43. Can clients request copyrighted characters, and if so, what boundaries do you want to establish?
44. Do you ship physical artwork or custom shoes? Where can you ship?
45. Should customers be able to pay through the site eventually, or should launch begin with inquiries and custom quotes?

### F. Personal story and trust

46. What made you start drawing?
47. What do you notice or care about in characters that other people might miss?
48. What do you want people to understand about your approach to representation?
49. Which parts of your personality should the website reveal beyond the artwork?
50. Are fitness, music, fashion, and everyday life part of the artistic story, or should they remain mainly on social media?
51. Do you have a portrait you love, or should we plan a small photo session?
52. Do you have testimonials or messages from happy commission clients we can request permission to use?

### G. Content, ownership, and maintenance

53. What name, pronouns, city/region, email address, and social links should be public?
54. Do you already own a domain name? If not, what names would you consider?
55. Do you have an existing logo or only the circular signature and illustrated avatar?
56. Who should add new artwork after launch?
57. How often do you realistically expect to update the portfolio?
58. Are you comfortable learning a simple content editor, or would you rather send updates to the designer?
59. Are there dates, launches, conventions, or commission windows that affect the schedule?
60. Are there accessibility needs or preferences we should account for beyond standard best practices?

### Reference evaluation — Lively Scout

**Reference:** <https://www.livelyscout.com/work>  
**Added:** July 2, 2026  
**Reaction:** The designer likes this direction and plans to send it to the artist for feedback.  
**Status:** Promising reference; artist reaction pending

Lively Scout is a useful structural reference because it lets saturated illustration carry the experience. Its broader site also separates polished portfolio work from a “Play” area for experiments, introduces the artist with concise positioning, and makes collaboration contact easy to find.

#### Elements worth borrowing

- Artwork-first presentation with large, confident imagery
- A clean portfolio grid that can accommodate varied proportions
- Minimal navigation and interface competition
- Clear distinction between professional work and playful experiments
- Short, personality-rich artist introduction
- Direct commission/collaboration call to action
- Project pages that can provide context beyond an Instagram tile
- Enough visual restraint for each illustration to establish its own palette

#### How to translate it into Art Prodigy's identity

- Replace the retro botanical/psychedelic tone with cartoon, comic, anime, gaming, and Black pop-cultural energy.
- Treat portfolio tiles like comic covers, episodes, channels, or collectible cards without sacrificing clarity.
- Use his circular signature as an issue stamp, category badge, or transition mark.
- Replace soft decorative motifs with restrained halftones, speed lines, ink borders, glow effects, and sticker-like labels.
- Use his illustrated self-portrait as the host or artist introduction if he approves.
- Rename a potential “Play” section in his voice—for example, **Sketchbook**, **Bonus Level**, **The Lab**, or **After Credits**.
- Use categories relevant to his work: Originals, Remixes/Fan Art, Culture, Commissions, and Custom Kicks.
- Keep motion small and characterful: card tilt, power-up glow, panel reveal, or character reaction.

#### What not to copy

- The other artist's exact grid composition, typography, color palette, wording, or decorative motifs
- A polished commercial-client posture that overstates Art Prodigy's current services or client history
- Product-shop functionality unless Art Prodigy confirms products and fulfillment are ready
- Visual effects that make every artwork look as though it belongs to one forced palette

#### Recommended remix

Use Lively Scout's **content hierarchy and confidence**, not its identity. The proposed Art Prodigy version would retain a simple Work / About / Sketchbook / Commissions backbone, then layer in his own comic-cartoon visual language. This provides a calmer and more achievable interpretation of “The Saturday Morning Universe” than turning every part of the interface into a themed interaction.

#### Questions to ask when sharing the reference

1. What is your immediate reaction to this site?
2. Do you like how large and prominent the artwork is?
3. Does the clean layout feel right, or would you want more comic/cartoon personality around it?
4. Do you like the separation between polished “Work” and experimental “Play”?
5. Would you want your projects organized by category, or shown as one curated collection?
6. Which parts feel like you, and which parts definitely do not?

## 18. Update protocol

After each meaningful conversation or design review:

1. Add the date and a short entry to the decision and revision log.
2. Replace answered assumptions with the artist's own words.
3. Mark decisions as **proposed**, **approved**, **rejected**, or **deferred**.
4. Add new requirements to the relevant section rather than only to meeting notes.
5. Preserve rejected directions briefly so future collaborators understand why they were rejected.
6. Record who owns every next action and any agreed deadline.
